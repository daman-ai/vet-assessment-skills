# Short modes

The full six-phase uplift is a large engagement run once. These are what keeps it
alive afterwards. They read the same ledger; they do not start a new one.

## Monthly pulse

One page. **Exceptions only — do not report what is fine.** The whole value is
that a page with two lines on it gets read in thirty seconds, and a page with
thirty lines gets filed.

Report:

- Obligations falling due in the next 60 days that have no evidence of being
  started
- Trainer credential and industry currency expiring in the next 90 days, per
  trainer, per product — with the products that would stop
- Complaints and appeals past their internal timeframe, by age
- Students identified at risk with no documented intervention, by days elapsed
- **Any CRICOS course or registered location approaching 9 months since its last
  overseas-student delivery day**
- Superseded products with live enrolments and less than 6 months on the
  transition clock

The CRICOS delivery line is the one to put first when it fires. Everything else
on the page is a finding waiting to happen; that one is a registration ending
automatically.

Where an exception has appeared for three consecutive months, say so. A recurring
exception is not an exception, it is a broken control, and it belongs in the
quarterly with a control-effectiveness downgrade.

## Quarterly health check

Two pages. Traffic-light table, three actions for this quarter, and what can
safely wait.

Run each leading indicator in `03-systems-design.md` against this quarter's
actual data. Then answer the question the client actually has: **what is trending
toward a finding in the next two quarters?** That is a different question from
what is non-compliant today, and it is the one a quarterly exists to answer. An
indicator moving in the wrong direction while still inside its threshold is the
most useful thing on the page.

Also: check the calendar for anything due or missed; check the automatic
cancellation clock for every CRICOS course at every registered location; update
control effectiveness in the risk register where the evidence has changed.

Cite the evidence for each light. Flag anything that could not be verified —
usually because a register was not exported — and name what is needed.

Three actions. Not eight. A quarterly with eight actions produces none.

## Regulatory change

Triggered by a change being announced or coming into force.

Verify it against primary sources **first** — the Federal Register of
Legislation, the Department of Education, ASQA — and record the date checked.
Sector commentary is a useful pointer to what changed and an unreliable source
for what it says.

Then, for each entity:

1. What actually changes — distinguishing what is newly mandatory from what is
   newly recommended
2. Which documents and systems are affected, by document id from the register
3. What must be done, by when, by whom
4. What to do early to be ahead of it

A dated action plan with owners. Not a summary of the change — the client can
read the ASQA newsletter themselves, and what they cannot do is work out which of
their eleven policies now says something wrong.

Where the change is not yet law, say so plainly and give the expected commencement
with its status. Announced, introduced, passed and commenced are four different
things, and RTOs have rewritten policies against bills that were later amended.

## Pre-audit rehearsal

Not in the original method; add it when a performance assessment is scheduled or
likely.

Take the evidence architecture and run it as a request list: for each requirement
in scope, produce the artefact **now**, from where it actually lives, timed. What
takes more than an hour to find does not exist for practical purposes, and that
is the finding.

This surfaces a failure mode nothing else catches: evidence that exists, is
correct, and is unretrievable — in a departed staff member's mailbox, on a
personal drive, in a system nobody has credentials for any more. It rates as a
3 in the gap analysis and behaves like a 1 in the room.
