# Deliverable specifications

Section-by-section for each of the eight files. Page counts are targets, not limits — but a facilitator guide over eight pages will not be read, and a worksheet over two pages will not be finished in the time allowed.

## 1. Facilitator Guide

Five to seven pages. Facilitator only — it carries the model answers and the resolutions, so it must never be printed for participants. Say so in the delivery message.

**Page 1 — orientation.** Title block, then a details table: audience, duration, group size (with what to do above and below the range), standards or framework covered, the worked tool and why it was chosen, room set-up. Then a short "why this session, why now" passage and a comparison table if the session turns on a distinction people routinely collapse. Close the page with a callout of the one line to say out loud in the room.

**Page 2 — outcomes and run sheet.** Four learning outcomes, each written as something a participant can do afterwards, not something they will "understand". Then the run sheet: time, slide numbers, segment, and what the facilitator actually does. Then a "what to have ready" list distinguishing printed-per-pair from printed-per-person, plus anything to write on the board before people arrive and anything not to hand out yet.

**Pages 3–4 — segment notes.** One page per teaching segment. Talking points as bullets with a bolded lead phrase. An "anticipate this question" callout with the question and the answer. A short "what not to spend time on" section naming the adjacent topics that will pull the conversation sideways, and what to do when someone raises them.

**Page 5 — Activity 1.** How to run it, as numbered steps with minute markers. Then the model findings in a table: what they are against, the defect, what has to change. Mark clearly that these are held back until the debrief. Close with a callout on handling defensiveness.

**Page 6 — Activity 2, close, recording.** How to run it and how to run the split. Then the resolution in a callout, stated in full — including the reason most people will give that is wrong, and why. Then the three closing commitments, and what to do with the paperwork within the week.

## 2. Slide deck

Fourteen to eighteen slides, 16:9. Speaker notes on every slide, carrying what is not on the slide.

Structure as a sandwich: dark title, light content, dark section dividers, dark close. Section dividers carry a two-digit number token and separate the four movements of the session.

Two or three slides should be dark full-bleed statements — one line of argument, large, with a short line under it. These are the slides people remember, and they give the facilitator permission to stop and let something land.

Content slides work as: kicker line, title, then either a two-column comparison, a 2×2 card grid, or three stacked rows. Do not mix more than two of these patterns in one deck.

Match slide numbers to the facilitator guide's run sheet. Verify at the end.

## 3. Activity 1 worksheet

Two pages, one per pair.

**Page 1.** Title block. An identification table with space for the pair, the date, and their allocation. A short "what you are doing" passage that names the obligation being discharged and, just as importantly, what they are *not* doing. The yardstick itself as a compact reference table — they will look at it the whole session. Then a numbered list of questions that find defects fastest, each written as an instruction to carry out on the tool rather than a thing to consider.

**Page 2.** A three-row table defining what a finding must contain. Then two finding blocks. Each block: what it was reviewed against, the defect and exactly where it is, the requirement it breaches, what has to change, and a blocking decision as two tick boxes with the consequence spelled out on each.

Use fixed row heights for the answer boxes. Do not use ruled dotted lines — see `build-conventions.md`.

## 4. Activity 2 worksheet

Two pages, one per person. This activity works by surprise, so the sheet must not be handed out early and must not be readable ahead.

**Page 1.** Title block. A "read this before you start" callout instructing silence and no conferring, and stating plainly that the activity is not a test of the participant. Then the stimulus — reproduce it exactly as the learner or user received it, in a bordered box, and state explicitly what the tool does *not* provide alongside it. Then the response to be judged, in a distinct tinted box, with a word count or equivalent metric stated underneath. Close with three neutral questions to hold in mind while marking — neutral, because a leading question destroys the split.

**Page 2.** Three marking boxes: what was done correctly, what is missing or wrong, and the feedback they would actually write. Then the judgement row with two tick boxes and one line for the deciding factor. Then a visible STOP rule across the page. Then three after-the-discussion boxes, the middle one of which asks where in the tool the disagreement would be settled — the answer is nowhere, and that is the finding. Close with a callout carrying the transferable principle.

## 5. Attendance and evaluation record

One page, one per participant. Per-participant rather than a shared sign-in sheet, because the evaluation half is individual and the sheet has to feed each person's PD plan.

Blocks in order: session details (title, date, facilitator, duration, standards covered, currency type); participant details (name, role, units they assess, signature); evaluation as four self-rating statements against a 1–5 scale, each statement phrased as a capability rather than a satisfaction question; three short free-text boxes, one of which asks them to name a tool of their own that needs the treatment — that answer is worth more than the ratings; and a tinted "for office use" strip capturing the continuous improvement reference and confirmation the attendance was copied to the PD plan.

Keep it to one page. A two-page evaluation form does not get completed.

## 6. Group allocation cards

One page per group. Splits a body of criteria across four groups so that each criterion is examined by exactly two groups.

With four principles and four rules, this allocation gives complete double coverage:

| Group | Principles | Rules of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| A | Validity, Reliability | Valid, Sufficient |
| B | Fairness, Flexibility | Authentic, Current |
| C | Validity, Fairness | Sufficient, Current |
| D | Reliability, Flexibility | Valid, Authentic |

Double coverage is deliberate: nothing goes unexamined, and when two groups independently surface the same defect the facilitator has the strongest possible evidence that the method works rather than the reviewer being clever. Say this on the card.

Each card: a header band with the group letter in a filled tile, then two sections. Every entry carries three things — what it requires, what a breach looks like, and a specific instruction to carry out on the tool. Close with a reminder of the deliverable and the deadline.

Note that these cards will duplicate any prompt questions already on the worksheet. Tell the user, and offer to strip that section from the worksheet since the cards do the job better by being targeted per group.

## 7. Model answer copies

Structural clones of the blank worksheets. See the "Model answer copies" section of SKILL.md for the method.

**Activity 1 model.** Fill the allocation fields with one group's allocation so it reads as a real completed sheet rather than a generic answer key. Two worked findings, each demonstrating the three-part structure. Give each blocking decision a one-line justification in red beneath the tick.

If both findings are blocking — which is honest for most real packs — say so in the delivery message and offer to swap one for a non-blocking example, so participants see the distinction drawn rather than described.

**Activity 2 model.** Fill the three marking boxes, tick the judgement, give the deciding factor in one line, and complete the three after-discussion boxes. The prediction about how the room will split should be labelled as typical rather than guaranteed, and should name every position that will appear — including the wrong one that sounds reasonable.
